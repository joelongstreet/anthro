E.O.J.

A few years ago, I got a phone call from my mom. My dad had just had his second heart attack. He was in the hospital, unconscious and unresponsive, and things weren’t good. I thought this might be the last time I’d see him.

My daughter Jane was three at the time. She could tell something was wrong, and while I was rushing around the house packing a bag, she asked me what was going on. I told her Papa’s heart was broken and I had to go see him. She nodded and said she knew how to fix it, then wandered off while I kept packing.

Right before I walked out the door, Jane handed me a drawing. “This is Egee,” she told me, very sure of herself. “It will fix Papa’s heart.” I didn’t really know what to say, so I kissed her on the head, stuffed the paper in my pocket, and left.

Somewhere on the highway, thinking about my kids, my dad, and everything else, it hit me. Jane had drawn E.O.J. (Joe spelled backwards). When I was about Jane’s age, my dad made up an imaginary friend for me named E.O.J., and he told me stories about his adventures. I hadn’t realized he’d been telling Jane those same stories.

When I got to the hospital, nobody knew how the night would go. My mom and sisters were close to each other. When no one was looking, I pulled the crumpled drawing out of my pocket, unfolded it, and showed it to my dad.

I’ll never know for sure, but I think Jane was right. That picture fixed Papa’s heart.

Years later, Papa is doing well. Better than well, actually. He and my mom are retired and living their dream, traveling the world, looking after small farms all around the United States, and taking their grandkids along on trips whenever they can.

To me, E.O.J. has become a symbol of perseverance and creativity, two things my dad instilled in me. I’ve carried this exact picture of him in my head since I was a little boy. I don’t remember ever drawing him as a kid. I guess I never needed to. I knew what he looked like and I knew what he meant to me.

The background and frame E.O.J. is mounted on are made from pieces of my dad’s desk. It’s an old captain’s desk from a ship, one his own parents refinished for him, and he worked at it the whole time I was growing up. It’s a small reminder of something else he taught me: how much it means to provide for your family, and how those things get handed down.

Now that my kids are getting older and starting to understand life a little more, it feels like the right time for them to meet the E.O.J. I’ve always seen, and to know what he means to me.
